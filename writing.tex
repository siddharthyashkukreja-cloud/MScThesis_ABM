\paragraph{Margin is procyclical, and it clusters.}
Posted initial margin sits at its $4\%$ anti-procyclicality floor in calm and ramps to an $11.4\%$ peak
in stress ($2.84\times$), averaging $7.7\%$ over the window (Figure~\ref{fig:res-im}). The
procyclicality is built into the volatility-sensitive VaR margin --- it is the
\citet{brunnermeier_pedersen_2009} margin--funding channel by construction --- but the model reveals its
\emph{incidence}: clients post roughly half of all initial margin (${\approx}50\%$ under stress,
Figure~\ref{fig:res-imtype}), so the client tier this thesis adds is a first-order driver of the
system's collateral demand, not a passive add-on \citep{menkveld_vuillemey_2021}. And margin calls do
not arrive evenly --- they cluster, with baseline intensity and bursts both amplified under stress
(Figure~\ref{fig:res-mc}), the emergent counterpart of the price's volatility clustering and the
destabilising-spike concern of \citet{glasserman_wu_2018}.

\section{Margin Procyclicality}\label{sec:res-h1}

The central finding is that margin does not \emph{remove} default risk so much as \emph{relocate} it. As the
standing margin rises from flat-$4\%$ to flat-$12\%$, client defaults fall steadily ($14.8 \to 6.9$ per
seed) --- more collateral means fewer clients exhaust their cash --- but member defaults \emph{rise}
($0.01 \to 0.55$ per seed) and the mutualised default fund, never touched at flat-$4\%$, is reached in
$42\%$ of seeds at flat-$12\%$. The mechanism is one of loss \emph{concentration}, not a drain on member
funding. A non-bank member posts no initial margin of its own --- it guarantees its clients' positions ---
so a higher standing margin does not lock up \emph{its} cash; what it changes is the \emph{shape} of client
failure. Raising the margin pushes clients to the capital floor sooner, so most freeze and default earlier
and \emph{smaller}, but a few are trapped at the floor holding large positions that, when they finally
default, are assumed by the carrying member and closed out into the falling market. Higher margin thus thins
the body but fattens the tail of the client default-size distribution, and it is the rare oversized
close-out --- a position the surviving members cannot fully absorb --- that exhausts a thinly-capitalised
non-bank member and reaches the pooled fund --- the few large, imbalanced peripheral clients that
\citet{paddrik_rajan_young_2020} find drive most of the contagion in a cleared network. The result is a
risk \emph{transformation} rather than a risk reduction, in the sense of \citet{cont_2017} and
\citet{biais_heider_hoerova_2016}: client failures are converted into rarer but larger member-level and
mutualised losses. This relocation is an emergent property, not a design artefact: no rule in the model
ties client defaults to member defaults: it arises only from the interaction of the freeze floor with
the close-out of the few oversized positions it leaves behind. Mutualisation runs
through this member channel specifically: the waterfall reaches the pooled fund only when a clearing member
itself fails --- invariably a thinly-capitalised non-bank member --- whose clients are ported and whose
residual deficit ($\approx\$1.2$B per failure) is met first by its own default-fund contribution
($\approx\$0.4$B) and the CCP's skin-in-the-game ($\approx\$0.36$B), with $\approx\$0.48$B drawn from the
surviving members' mutualised fund (waterfall level~$3$; L4--L5 never engage) --- whereas client defaults
alone are absorbed at the carrying member and never mutualise.

Against this backdrop the reactive rule is attractive. At the same $\approx\!8\%$ average margin its
risk-metric means are essentially flat-$8\%$'s --- client defaults ($10.1$ vs $11.1$), member defaults
($0.33$ vs $0.35$), mutualisation ($25\%$ vs $30\%$ of seeds) --- so even over $150$ seeds reactive
\emph{matches} flat-$8\%$'s stress protection rather than beating it; the arm-to-arm gaps are within
sampling noise. What is unambiguous is the \emph{cost}. Reactive front-loads collateral into the crash
trough, when clients are actually failing, rather than spreading it evenly across the window, holding the
flat-$4\%$ \emph{calm} cost ($\approx\$18$B, at the floor) against flat-$8\%$'s $\$32$B. Reactive's cost is
therefore neither coverage nor standing funding but the \emph{procyclical spike} itself: posted margin jumps
to a $\$38$B peak in the stress (Figure~\ref{fig:res-h1}, right), the collateral-demand shock the
procyclicality literature warns of.

Two cautions frame the result. First, it is a collateral-demand statement, \emph{not} crash amplification:
the fundamental path is exogenous and identical across all arms, so the drawdown does not respond to the
margining rule and margin cannot feed back into price here --- the channel is funding and loss-allocation,
in the spirit of \citet{brunnermeier_pedersen_2009} and \citet{murphy_vasios_vause_2014}, not a margin-driven
fire-sale spiral (Section~\ref{sec:res-limits}). Second, the member and mutualisation effects, though
directionally robust across the flat ladder ($p<0.0001$ from flat-$4\%$ to flat-$12\%$), are modest in
absolute terms at COVID severity (well under one member default per seed) and rest on only five non-bank
members, so individual arm-to-arm gaps are noisy; they also depend on the fixed-percentage default fund and
on the close-out and porting mechanics that govern how a defaulted book is absorbed, and are best read as
sensitivity levers rather than point estimates. They become first-order only under deeper-than-COVID stress,
which the synthetic-path extension of Section~\ref{sec:res-limits} is built to probe.

\section{Mechanism and Robustness}\label{sec:res-mech}

The relocation of Section~\ref{sec:res-h1} raises two questions: \emph{why} higher margin trades client
defaults for member defaults, and \emph{how robust} the pattern is. The mechanism is a
\emph{freeze-trap}. Under a high flat margin a stressed client reaches its trading-freeze floor before
it exhausts its collateral, so it is frozen rather than defaulted: client defaults fall from $14.8$ to
$6.9$ per seed and the \emph{typical} defaulted position shrinks (median $870 \to 767$ contracts,
Figure~\ref{fig:mech-position}). But the same protection lets a few positions build up undisturbed, so
the \emph{tail} of defaulted positions fattens --- its $99$th percentile rises from $3{,}584$ to
$5{,}533$ contracts as the flat margin goes from $4\%$ to $12\%$. It is this rare, large position,
closed out into a falling market, that overwhelms the thin non-bank member carrying it. The reactive
arm avoids the trap, keeping both the count moderate ($10.1$) and the tail thin ($99$th percentile
$3{,}042$): it raises margin only when volatility actually rises, rather than standing high enough to
freeze the small defaults out.

Figure~\ref{fig:mech-waterfall} traces where the resulting loss goes. As the flat margin rises the
per-failure deficit climbs the waterfall --- the defaulter's own default-fund contribution, then the
CCP's skin-in-the-game, then the mutualised pooled fund --- and reaches the pooled fund increasingly
often, from essentially never under flat-$4\%$ to $0.54$ events per seed under flat-$12\%$. The
surviving-member assessment (L4) and the CCP's own capital (L5) are implemented but never engage at
these severities, so losses are absorbed within the first three layers.

Two further cuts characterise the client tier. Defaults fall on fundamental and momentum clients far
more than on noise traders (Figure~\ref{fig:mech-type}): the directional traders carry inventory that
moves against them in stress, whereas the zero-intelligence flow is roughly balanced. And client
\emph{trading freezes} are almost entirely a contagion phenomenon --- $98$--$99\%$ of frozen
client-time is caused by the clearing member withdrawing clearing services after breaching its own
capital ratio, not by the client's own distress (Figure~\ref{fig:mech-freezes}). The client tier's
fragility is therefore dominated by loss of access to clearing through its member, the contagion
channel this model was built to expose, rather than by clients failing on their own account.

\paragraph{Robustness.} The relocation trend is large and monotone across $150$ seeds and highly
significant ($p < 0.0001$ for the client-default decline). The member-default and mutualisation counts,
by contrast, are rare events spread over five non-bank members, so individual arm-to-arm differences
are noisy: the reactive-versus-flat-$8\%$ gaps in particular sit within sampling noise (Welch
$p \approx 0.12$--$0.19$), which is why the reactive result is framed as cost-efficiency rather than a
strict risk improvement. The findings are conditional on a single historical stress path --- the
exogenous-fundamental caveat the synthetic ensemble of Section~\ref{sec:res-synth} is built to relax ---
and on the set-by-design clearing parameters (the house leverage cap, the fixed-percentage default
fund, and the close-out recovery rate), whose levels shift the absolute default counts but not the
direction of the relocation.

The experiments so far replay a single historical fundamental per regime, so a fair question is whether the
findings are an artefact of that one realisation. The model is built to answer it: the fundamental can be
regenerated from the jointly-calibrated two-factor stochastic-volatility process of
Section~\ref{sec:fundamental}, so the cleared market is re-instantiated on an ensemble of $N=80$ unseen ES
stress paths --- a Monte Carlo over calibrated scenarios rather than a single replay. Each path drifts down at
the empirical COVID-window rate but draws its own volatility from the calibrated fast and slow log-volatility
factors, so the ensemble spans a range of realised volatilities around a common decline
(Figure~\ref{fig:synth-paths}).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{Figures/synth_paths.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Synthetic two-factor-SV stress-path ensemble.} $N=80$ independent synthetic ES fundamental
  paths (thin) with the ensemble mean (bold), drifting down at the empirical COVID-window rate. The spread
  around the declining mean is the scenario distribution the single historical path cannot provide.}
  \label{fig:synth-paths}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The market layer generalises.}
Run on these unseen paths, the simulated mid still reproduces the ES stylised facts --- the heavy tails and,
crucially, the slow volatility clustering the two-factor log-volatility was built for
(Chapter~\ref{ch:calibration}, Figure~\ref{fig:cal-synth-facts}; Table~\ref{tab:cal-synth}). The calibration
is therefore not an artefact of the single historical realisation, and the clustering it earns is what drives
the clearing dynamics below.

\paragraph{The clearing layer is robust on the median path, with a volatility-driven tail.}
Read from the clearing side, the ensemble both confirms the single-path result and extends it
(Table~\ref{tab:synth-clearing}, Figure~\ref{fig:synth-clearing}). On the typical path the system is benign:
the median path produces $3$ client defaults, no member default, and never draws the mutualised default fund
--- the loss localisation of the reactive base case (Section~\ref{sec:res-general}), now reproduced on
scenarios the model never saw. But the ensemble reveals what one path cannot. A tail of paths exhausts the
member tier: $14$ of $80$ draw the mutualised default fund (L$\geq3$), $8$ reach surviving-member loss-sharing
(L4, the contagion channel), and $3$ reach CCP capital (L5), with $77$ member defaults in total concentrated
on these paths.

\paragraph{Volatility, not the drawdown, drives the tail --- and margin calls cluster.}
What separates the benign bulk from the tail is realised volatility, not the depth of the decline: clearing
stress correlates strongly with a path's volatility (client and member defaults $r\approx0.7$, peak posted
margin $r\approx0.9$) but barely with its net drawdown ($r\approx0$). The channel is procyclical margin
(Figure~\ref{fig:synth-clearing}, right): posted initial margin ramps roughly nine-fold from floor to peak,
consistently across all $80$ paths, but the calls do not arrive evenly --- within a path the busiest $10\%$ of
hours carry $62\%$ of all margin called. This clustered, procyclical collateral demand is the emergent
counterpart of the fundamental's volatility clustering --- the same mechanism behind the empirical margin-call
clustering of Section~\ref{sec:res-general} (Figure~\ref{fig:res-mc}) --- and instantiates the destabilising
margin-spike concern of \citet{glasserman_wu_2018} and the \citet{brunnermeier_pedersen_2009} margin--funding
channel across an entire calibrated scenario set.

The ensemble thus turns the single-shock study into a severity statement: the member tier absorbs the median
calibrated crash, but a non-trivial tail of high-volatility paths exhausts it into mutualisation. Locating
that severity frontier --- the probability of a member default or a mutualised draw as a function of stress
--- is the probabilistic question the single replay could not pose, and the natural next step
(Section~\ref{sec:res-limits}).

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis asked how a central counterparty's margin and default-management machinery behaves once an
explicit client-clearing tier is added, and in particular how \emph{margin procyclicality} shapes where
losses fall in a crash. To answer it we built,
calibrated, and validated an agent-based model of the CCP-cleared E-mini~S\&P~500 futures market: a
discrete call-auction limit order book of fundamentalist, momentum, and zero-intelligence traders
\citep{chiarella_iori_perello_2009, farmer_2005, cont_stoikov_talreja_2008} coupled to a two-tier clearing
structure --- a CCP, ten banking and five non-banking clearing members, and ninety clients --- with
procyclical value-at-risk initial margin, capital-ratio constraints, a cover-2 default fund, and a
five-level default waterfall. The clearing architecture extends \citet{deloitte_ccp} and
\citet{simudyne_odd} with the explicit client tier that \citet{menkveld_vuillemey_2021} identify as an open
question, and the market layer was calibrated to the empirical ES stylised facts by surrogate-assisted
simulated moments \citep{franke_westerhoff_2012, lamperti_2018, gao_2022_hfabm}, reproducing the heavy tails
and volatility clustering of \citet{cont_2001} across a calm and a COVID-stress regime. The calibrated,
validated model is itself the thesis's primary contribution: to our knowledge it is the first agent-based
model to couple a microstructurally realistic order book to a complete CCP default waterfall with a client
tier.

\paragraph{The calibrated model behaves like a real clearing system under stress.}
Run on the historical COVID path, the clearing tier reproduces the qualitative pattern of March~2020: the
members ride the $8\%$ capital floor --- about three of the five client-clearing banks deleverage their own
books and roughly two of the five non-banks stop out per seed --- but they \emph{survive}; the $\approx 8.1$
client defaults per stressed seed are absorbed at the carrying member, with the defaulter's own margin
seized first. Loss is therefore relocated onto member balance sheets rather than erased
\citep{galbiati_soramaki_2013}, and the mutualised fund is reached only through the rarer member failure ---
a thinly-capitalised non-bank member roughly one stressed seed in five or six, whose residual deficit escalates
past its own resources and the CCP's skin-in-the-game to draw only $\sim\$0.05$B of the pooled fund. This is
consistent with the consensus reading of March~2020 as a margin and funding event rather than a
clearing-member solvency event \citep{esrb_2020, cont_2017}: the model transforms counterparty risk into
liquidity risk, exactly as \citet{cont_2017} describe.

\paragraph{Margin procyclicality relocates risk; it does not remove it.}
The headline experiment replaced the reactive VaR margin with flat margins of $4\%$, $8\%$, and $12\%$. As
the standing margin rises, client defaults fall steadily ($14.8 \to 6.9$ per seed), but member defaults rise
($0.01 \to 0.55$) and the mutualised fund, never reached at flat-$4\%$, is drawn in $42\%$ of seeds at
flat-$12\%$ --- while the standing collateral cost climbs from $\$18$B to $\$42$B. Higher margin thus buys
client protection at the price of member-level and system-level fragility, but through loss
\emph{concentration} rather than a funding drain: a non-bank member posts no margin of its own, so a higher
standing margin does not lock up \emph{its} liquidity --- it reshapes client failure. More margin forces
clients to the capital floor sooner, so most freeze and default smaller, while a few are trapped holding
large positions that, on default, are assumed by the carrying member and closed out into the falling market;
the rare oversized close-out is what exhausts a thin non-bank member and reaches the pooled fund --- a risk
\emph{transformation} in the sense of \citet{cont_2017} and \citet{biais_heider_hoerova_2016}, client
failures converted into rarer but larger member-level and mutualised losses. Against this backdrop the
\emph{reactive} rule is attractive: at the same average margin it \emph{matches} flat-$8\%$'s stress
protection at the cheap flat-$4\%$ standing cost, front-loading collateral into the crash trough instead of
holding it all year. Its risk-metric means sit at or below flat-$8\%$ but, over $40$ seeds and only five
non-bank members, the gaps are within sampling noise, so the robust advantage is cost rather than a strict
risk improvement; that cost is concentrated in the procyclical \emph{spike} the regulatory literature warns
of \citep{glasserman_wu_2018, esrb_2020, blanck_2025}. Because the price path is exogenous, this is a
collateral-demand result --- the margin--funding strain of \citet{brunnermeier_pedersen_2009} and the
leverage cycle of \citet{aymanns_farmer_2015, thurner_2012} --- and not a claim about margin-driven price
amplification.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several caveats bound these findings, and motivate the extensions below. First, each regime is driven by a
single fixed historical fundamental path, so the seeds vary only the agent RNG: the experiments measure the
clearing response to a \emph{replayed} crash, not path or scenario uncertainty. Second, and relatedly,
because that path is exogenous the model captures the collateral-demand and loss-allocation channels but
\emph{not} a feedback loop in which margin calls force sales that move the price and trigger further calls;
the procyclicality result should be read as a collateral-demand and loss-allocation statement, not as crash
amplification. Third, the system is
stressed only at the realised COVID drawdown ($-25.6\%$), at which member failures and mutualisation are
rare; the severity at which the member tier's protection is systematically exhausted is not mapped here.
Fourth, volatility clustering is reproduced less tightly than the heavy tails: the synthetic
extension's two-timescale stochastic-volatility fundamental is still a finite sum of exponentials that only
approximates the power-law volatility memory of the data \citep{barndorff_shephard_2001}, and the
order book further attenuates the mid-price autocorrelation. A related point is that the calibration
and validation are of the market layer with the clearing tier inactive --- the price-generating process
to which the clearing overlay is then applied; activating the tier introduces the forced-liquidation
feedback the model is built to study, which itself perturbs the stressed return dynamics (a short-lag
positive autocorrelation and a heavier tail), so the full clearing-on model is not itself re-fit to the
stylised facts. Finally, several structural quantities --- the
$15\%$ house-margin cap, the position limits, the member capital scales, and the close-out recovery and
porting capacity that govern how a defaulted book is absorbed --- are set by design rather than estimated,
and the high-margin mutualisation result additionally relies on a fixed-percentage default fund (the
loss-over-margin fund mechanically vanishes once flat margin exceeds the stress move); all are best treated
as sensitivity levers rather than point estimates. Relatedly, the model's ten well-capitalised member banks
hold larger proprietary books, relative to the ninety-client population, than real dealers, so posted margin
splits roughly evenly between house and client accounts rather than the $\approx\!70\%$ client share
observed empirically \citep{ofr_2026}; because the crash path is fundamentalist-pinned this is a calibration
caveat that does not affect the loss-allocation results.

\paragraph{Future work.}
The model is built to relax the first two limitations directly. Its fundamental can be regenerated from a
two-factor stochastic-volatility process --- jointly recalibrated with the agents to the ES stylised facts ---
turning the single-shock study into a Monte~Carlo over an ensemble of plausible ES stress paths; this converts the qualitative findings into probabilistic
statements --- the probability of a member default or a mutualised draw as a function of stress severity ---
and a severity sweep over that ensemble is the natural next step. Beyond that, endogenising the price so that
forced close-outs feed back into it would let the model speak to margin-driven amplification rather than
collateral demand alone, and estimating the structural knobs would tighten the quantitative claims. More
broadly, the calibrated clearing sandbox can be re-used to stress-test any margin, default-fund, or capital
rule against a realistic cleared market --- the contribution we regard as most durable, beyond the specific
procyclicality result above.
